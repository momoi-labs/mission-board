# Ideal work

Help the person discover what work they want more of, under what conditions,
and what they want to explore next. Ground the result in their own experiences.
This exercise can end in a private reflection or a text for another reader.

## Establish the purpose

Use the conversation so far before asking for more context. Identify whether
the person wants personal reflection, a text to share, or both. For a shared
text, establish its audience and purpose. Confirm the output language when
it is unclear; the interview can stay in the person's conversational language.

Offer the relevant output formats below when length has not been specified.
If the person has no preference, state that you will use the standard format.
They can change the format later without restarting the interview.

## Interview

Ask one focused question at a time. Accept fragments, voice transcripts,
contradictions, and uncertainty as useful material. Start from an actual
experience when possible, such as a day that left them tired but satisfied.
Follow what their answer reveals instead of exhausting a questionnaire.

Keep track of these distinctions within the conversation:

- Experience: what the person has actually done, with supporting examples.
- Preference: what they want to spend more or less time doing.
- Requirement: what a suitable role must provide and what they merely prefer.
- Exploration: interests and possibilities they have not yet tried or decided.

Use these questions selectively to fill meaningful gaps:

- What were you doing during work you particularly enjoyed? Which part mattered?
- Which tasks are you good at but would rather do less of?
- How would you divide your time in an ideal week? Rough priorities are enough.
- What makes a problem worth solving for you? Who benefits from the result?
- How do you want to collaborate, make decisions, and relate to users?
- How much implementation, technical leadership, or people management do you want?
- What would make an otherwise attractive role exhausting or unsuitable?
- What must be present now, and what would you like to grow toward later?
- What would you like to try next, and what would that help you learn?

Briefly reflect back patterns when they change the understanding of the person.
Treat interpretations as provisional. Expertise in an activity is not evidence
that they want it to dominate their work. When answers appear to conflict,
clarify the activity, context, or time horizon before choosing an interpretation.
Apply corrections throughout subsequent drafts.

Discuss working conditions in concrete terms. Translate a preference such as
"time to think" into what the person needs room to do. Describe communication
and support preferences without inferring diagnoses or causes. For shared text,
include personal health details only when the person wants them included.

Draft when you can describe the desired activities, supporting experiences,
working environment, meaningful limits, and direction of growth. An explicit
"I don't know yet" can remain an open question. If the person asks for a draft
sooner, use the available material and preserve uncertainty rather than
continuing the interview.

## Choose the output

| Format | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| Short | Two or three paragraphs covering the main preference, evidence, and working conditions. |
| Standard | Four or five paragraphs with examples, priorities, and a direction of growth. |
| Complete | A structured reflection covering desired work, evidence, requirements, tolerable tradeoffs, future interests, and unresolved questions. |

Treat these lengths as guides. Follow an explicit word limit or requested
structure. Scale detail to the available evidence rather than padding the text.

For personal reflection, make unresolved choices visible. Close with a small
set of next objectives based on expressed interests. For each objective,
suggest a concrete next action or experiment and what the person could learn
from it. Label these as proposals until adopted. Separate what matters now
from longer-term ambitions; use dates only when the person supplies or requests
them.

For shared text, write in the first person and use examples that explain the
desired work. Keep private deliberation out unless it serves the stated
purpose. Describe limits honestly and constructively. Do not turn operating
experience into enthusiasm for operations, or exploration into an established
contribution. If both outputs are requested, keep the personal reflection and
the shareable text separate.

## Preserve accuracy and voice

Preserve the person's names for projects and technologies. Clarify ambiguous
names rather than substituting a familiar project. A conversation about a
possible contribution remains exploration until the person says it began.
Public sources can explain a project, but cannot prove someone's private
involvement. Keep achievements and credentials grounded in supplied evidence.

Use the person's vocabulary and concrete examples. Make the writing natural
and easy to scan. Keep interview turns brief and deliver the final copy without
process commentary. Use `unslop` when available for substantial prose editing;
the exercise should also work without that skill.

Before delivery, check that preferences match the latest corrections, examples
support the claims, and current experience is distinct from future interests.
Check the chosen purpose, language, and length. Include only the deliverables
requested, and stop when the person has a usable reflection or text.

## Keep personal material private

Keep the exercise in the conversation unless the person asks to save it.
Save personal reflections, career records, and generated texts only under
`~/.local/share/mission-board/`, outside this public repository, as defined in
[ADR-0004](../../../docs/adr/0004-career-data-in-xdg-data-home.md).
When saving career goals or updating the record, follow the formats and
confirmation steps in [career-record](../../../skills/career-record/SKILL.md).
The reusable skill and fictional examples belong in the repository;
participant details do not.
